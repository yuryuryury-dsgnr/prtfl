01 · I built the tool I kept needing

Problem & Context

I rebalance crypto myself, and the pair I actually care about is almost never one an exchange will show me. Everything is priced against USDT. If I want to know where SOL stands against XRP, no exchange and no wallet treats that as something I can keep on a list and watch.

02 · A gap I knew from the inside

Positioning

I work in this market as a user, so I mapped it from the inside rather than from a feature matrix. Two categories already own the space, and neither of them treats an ALT/ALT pair as a thing you can hold:

Exchanges & wallets

Price altcoins against USDT, USD, or BTC. An arbitrary ALT/ALT pair isn't an object you can name, keep, or attach an alert to.

Portfolio trackers

Report holdings in fiat and alert on single coins. The ratio between two of your own positions isn't treated as a thing worth watching.

The position I took Put the four together in one place: a custom ALT/ALT pair, an alert on the ratio, holdings that recalculate, and a simulator to plan the move. Each exists somewhere on its own. The combination is what nobody was offering.

The gap has an engineering explanation, not a marketing one: there is no direct ALT/ALT feed to consume. The ratio has to be assembled from two separate USD legs and divided — a real build cost, and the reason the space stays empty. That was the opening, so I kept the scope tight enough to hold it.

03 · Diagnose, plan, implement — one change at a time

The Process

I designed this in code rather than in Figma, so a decision and its implementation happened in the same place. That forces every choice to be specific enough to actually run — there is no mockup to hide an unresolved detail in.

Working at that speed only holds up with a method, so every change went through the same loop: Diagnose → Plan → Implement. Understand the current state first, agree a precise plan, then implement it surgically — one small, reviewable change at a time.

The hardest part wasn't the interface. Notification servers, and APIs that had to behave across different countries, were the real work — and that was unfamiliar territory for me. The loop is what made it survivable instead of a run of lucky guesses.

Coins started as a page pushed from the watchlist header. I promoted it to a tab once I noticed I kept coming back to my holdings rather than visiting them.

Alerts went the other way. It was briefly a fourth tab; I moved it back behind a bell. Three tabs is the product — alerts are somewhere I check, not somewhere I live.

Detail screens sit above the bar rather than inside it. Pushing one hides the bar, and the tabs you left get properly set aside instead of lingering underneath — a structural choice that also spared me a class of bug on hidden screens.

05 · Five paths, and the edge I drew

User Flows

Each flow came out of one question: how few decisions can this take? What follows are the paths I settled on — and, in the last one, the line where the product deliberately stops.

Screen Action System Decision End Sheet / off-app
  • Action Tap + From the watchlist
  • Sheet Token selector Search the catalogue
  • Action Pick base
  • Action Pick quote
    • If you picked them the wrong way round
      Action Swap sides Same pair, reversed — no need to start over
  • End Pair is live Tracked in the list from that moment

Two picks, no form. The pair is the only thing the app asks you to create, so I kept it to two decisions — and made the sides swappable instead of expecting people to get the order right first time.

  • Screen Pair detail Opened from the watchlist
  • Action New alert
  • Sheet Prefilled at the current ratio Opens on today's number, not on an empty field
  • Decision Notifications allowed?
    • Not yet
      System Permission banner Asks once, in place — the alert is still saved
    • Yes
      Action Set the threshold
      • End Alert armed

The prefill is the whole design. An empty field asks “what number?” — prefilled with where the ratio sits right now, it asks “how far from here?” — which is the question I always arrived with myself.

The permission branch saves the alert either way. Making the notification prompt a precondition would have meant losing the thing the person came to do.

  • Screen Pair detail Simulator lives here, not in the list
  • Action Enter amounts What you'd move
  • Decision More than two steps?
    • Within Free
      End See the outcome Before committing to anything
    • Beyond Free
      Sheet Paywall Pro: unlimited chain steps

The simulator answers “what would this do” without doing it. Free stops at two steps in a chain; deeper chains are where Pro begins — I put the paywall where the tool starts doing planning work, not at the front door.

  • Screen Coins Its own tab
  • Action Enter what you hold Edited in place, hideable
  • System Prices move
  • End Totals recalculate In the denomination you choose

The only flow here with no branch, and that's the point: Coins is a ledger of what you own. Anything that behaves like an exchange rate belongs to the simulator — keeping those apart is what stops the app from implying it can trade for you.

  • System Threshold crossed Ratio reaches your number
  • System Push delivered Even with the app closed
  • Screen Open and check Chart, holdings, simulator
  • Outside the app
  • Off-app Execute on the exchange Rebalancer never places the order

This is where I drew the product's edge. Rebalancer tells you the moment arrived and shows you the numbers; the trade happens on your exchange. Not a missing feature — a deliberate limit that keeps the app clear of custody, keys and order execution.

07 · Two price paths, because one wasn't allowed

Technical Architecture

Three problems behind the interface shaped the architecture.

Prices — two independent paths

Lane A  From the phone — residential IP
Binance → CoinGecko
Lane B  From the worker — data-centre IP
Binance403 CoinGeckorate-limited
OKX → Coinbase → Coinpaprika

Binance answers a phone but 403s a data centre.

ALT/ALT — assembled, not fetched

BASE / USD ÷ QUOTE / USD = BASE / QUOTE

No ALT/ALT feed exists — both legs priced in USD, then divided.

Threshold → push

  • System Scheduled run, every minute Cron trigger on the worker
  • System Price both legs, derive the ratio Lane B chain, same source for both legs where possible
  • Decision Crossed, and not already sent?
    • No — already fired, or still short of the line
      System Stay silent, wait a minute An alert disarms itself once fired, so nobody gets the same news twice
    • Yes
      System Push → phone → alert history Delivered whether or not the app is running

Background refresh is unreliable, so the schedule lives on the server.

08 · Building for a market that isn't in one country

Localization

The audience for an ALT/ALT tool isn't concentrated in one market, so English-only would have narrowed the position from the start. The translation itself is machine work — generated with AI, then passed through Gemini, which caught nuances the first pass missed.

The design problem was everything around it: every new country brings its own regional requirements, and the app and backend have to absorb them without the interface growing a settings screen per market. That, not the word count, is what took the time.

Too early for usage evidence, and I would rather say that than dress a launch up as traction. The 31-language rollout is still waiting on review. The next honest signal is whether anyone arms a second alert after their first one fires.

10 · What the build taught me

Reflection

What I'd do differently

Underestimating the backend was the main misstep. The interface came together fast; the servers, notification infrastructure, and region-specific APIs were where the real time went. Next time I'd scope the invisible parts first.

What I learned

The line between designer and developer is thinner than it used to be. With AI, I shipped something end-to-end that would normally take a team — but only because I worked with discipline, not shortcuts. AI accelerates the parts you understand and is useless for the parts you don't; the diagnose-plan-implement loop is what kept a solo build from becoming chaos. The best results came when I knew exactly what I wanted before asking.
